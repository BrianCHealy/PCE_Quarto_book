PCE Practicum Power for R

Sample size and power calculations

Goal: This practicum will go over how to complete sample size and power calculations for one and two sample t-tests in R.

One sample t-test

A common scenario in medical research is when a new set of patients is compared to a historical control group with known mean. As an example, assume that we know the mean score on a math test using traditional teaching techniques is 85 with a standard deviation of 20. We have a new program that we believe will improve performance and lead to a mean score of 95. We can recruit 50 students and would like to know the power we will have to detect our hypothesized difference.

In this scenario, we are collecting a single sample and we will compare the observed data to a hypothesized mean value using a one sample t-test. Remember for a study of this type, there are five pieces of information of interest:

  1. Type I error rate (alpha level)

  2. Difference between the means of the null and alternative hypotheses

  3. Standard deviation of the data

  4. Sample size

  5. Power

We need to know four of these five pieces of information to calculate the final piece. The type I error rate is almost always chosen to be 0.05 based on a two-sided test. The standard deviation is 20 based on the information above, and we will assume that the standard deviation in our new group is the same as in the historical data. The null hypothesis in this example is that the mean on the new program is equal to 85 (\(H_0: \mu_0=85\)), and the alternative hypothesis is that the mean is equal to 95 (\(H_A: \mu_A=95\)). The sample size is 50.

Given this information, we can calculate the power to detect the proposed difference using the power.t.test command in R.

For this command, we enter the difference between the groups (delta=(95-85)), the standard deviation (sd=20), the sample size (n=50), type I error rate (sig.level=0.05), the type of t-test (type=“one.sample”), and the direction of the alternative hypothesis (alternative=“two.sided”).

power.t.test(delta=(95-85), sd=20, n=50, sig.level=0.05,type="one.sample", alternative="two.sided")

     One-sample t test power calculation 

              n = 50
          delta = 10
             sd = 20
      sig.level = 0.05
          power = 0.9338976
    alternative = two.sided

The results show that the power for the proposed study is 93.4.

As a follow-up, we might be interested in the sample size required to have 80% power. For this, we can also use the power.t.test command, but we must specify the power rather than the sample size (power=0.8)

power.t.test(delta=(95-85), sd=20, power=0.8, sig.level=0.05,type="one.sample", alternative="two.sided")

     One-sample t test power calculation 

              n = 33.3672
          delta = 10
             sd = 20
      sig.level = 0.05
          power = 0.8
    alternative = two.sided

Since we always round up, R shows that the sample size required for 80% power is 34.

Note that the command to calculate the power or the sample size is the same. R will calculate either the power or sample size depending on what is not included in the power.t.test command.

Two sample t-test

The more common scenario in medical research is a comparison of two groups. An alternative approach to investigate the question from the previous study would be to compare one group of students taught with the traditional teaching technique and another group of students taught with the new technique. Let’s assume that the mean in the traditional teaching group is 85, the mean in the new technique group is 95 and the standard deviation in each group is 20.

In this scenario, we are collecting two samples and we will compare the mean in the two samples using a two sample t-test. Remember for a study of this type, there are five pieces of information of interest:

  1. Type I error rate (alpha level)
  2. Difference between the means of the null and alternative hypotheses
  3. Standard deviation in each group (we assume it is equal for this function)
  4. Sample size
  5. Power

We need to know four of these five pieces of information to calculate the final piece. The type I error rate is chosen to be 0.05 based on a two-sided test. The standard deviation is 20 in each group based on the information above, and it is reasonable to assume they are equal based on the information above. The mean in the traditional program is 85 and the postulated mean under the new program is 95. Therefore, the null hypothesis in this example is that the mean in each program is equal to 85 \((H_0: \mu_1-\mu_2=0)\), and the alternative hypothesis is that the mean in the new program is 10 units higher \((H_A: \mu_1-\mu_2=10)\). The desired power for this study is 80%.

Given this information, we can calculate the sample size required for 80% power to detect the proposed difference using the power.t.test command in R.

For this command, we enter the difference between the groups (delta=(95-85)), the standard deviation (sd=20), power (power=0.8), type I error rate (sig.level=0.05), type of t-test (type=“two.sample”), and the direction of the alternative hypothesis (alternative=“two.sided”).

power.t.test(delta=(95-85), sd=20, power=0.8, sig.level=0.05,type="two.sample", alternative="two.sided")

     Two-sample t test power calculation 

              n = 63.76576
          delta = 10
             sd = 20
      sig.level = 0.05
          power = 0.8
    alternative = two.sided

NOTE: n is number in *each* group

Since we should always round up, the results show that we will need 64 patients per group to detect the proposed difference. This corresponds to a total sample size for the study of 128.

Two sample test of proportions

We would like to the effect of a treatment on the recurrence of disease in mice. We will consider recurrence of disease as a dichotomous variable. The proportion of mice that will have a recurrence in the placebo group is 0.7. We hypothesize that the proportion of mice that will have a recurrence in the treated group is 0.4. How many mice per group will we need to have 80% power to detect the proposed difference using a two sample test of proportions/chi-square test?

To calculate the power for a comparison of two proportions, we need to use the power.prop.test command in R. For this command, we enter the proportions in the two groups (p1=0.4, p2=0.7), power (power=0.8), and type I error rate (sig.level=0.05). Note that we do not need to specify a standard deviation in this case because the standard deviation of a proportion is related to the proportion.

power.prop.test(p1=0.4, p2=0.7, sig.level=0.05, power=0.8)

     Two-sample comparison of proportions power calculation 

              n = 41.97025
             p1 = 0.4
             p2 = 0.7
      sig.level = 0.05
          power = 0.8
    alternative = two.sided

NOTE: n is number in *each* group
